**Writing practice down helps teams work the same way. But improvement often relies on someone trying a way that isn't written down yet. Most playbooks never settle that tension.**

When that tension stays unsettled, a playbook fails in one of two ways. Either nobody updates it, and it becomes a museum piece that people trust long after it has stopped being true. Or everyone has to follow it, and anyone with a better way has to fight it. Laminate your ways of working and you get the second failure fast. The document meant to spread good practice becomes the thing teams work around.

Toyota's car plants deal with this through standard work, a written description of the current best way to do each job. Teams follow it, and anyone who finds a better way tests it against the standard. If the new way does better, it becomes the standard. Without a standard, there is nothing to test an improvement against.

This only holds if two conditions are met. Every play is open to change, and says so. And there is a cheap, known path for a local improvement to become the new standard.

That path is the most important play in this playbook. It lives in [Govern](/playbooks/creating-playbooks/govern/), and everything else here feeds it. A playbook without that loop is a museum piece on day one, whatever its preface says.

The implication is uncomfortable but necessary: the writing is the least interesting part of creating a playbook. The system around the writing, sensing what to codify, extracting it honestly, and governing how it changes, is where playbooks succeed or fail.

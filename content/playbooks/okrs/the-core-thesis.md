OKRs are not a management tool. They are a management philosophy with a template attached.

That is the main point. The artefact - the Objective and the Key Results - is the visible part. Underneath sits a set of assumptions about how humans should be coordinated, how trust is built, how ambition is rewarded, and how decisions get made. Most adoption failure is the philosophy showing through the template.

Organisations with a coordination philosophy built on trust, autonomy, and shared intent find that OKRs amplify good work. Their OKRs radiate the team's intent and anchor the team's decisions. Organisations with a coordination philosophy built on control, surveillance, and individual accountability find that OKRs amplify the same: more surveillance, faster, with a template that makes it look principled.

The implication: you cannot adopt OKRs without also adopting the philosophy the artefact assumes. And the philosophy is rarely named in the rollout. It is inherited, silently, from whichever interpretation of OKRs the implementer brings with them.

This means the work of "rolling out OKRs" is not primarily a writing exercise. It is a question of which management philosophy your organisation is willing to commit to. The template is often the most boring part.

The rest of this playbook is concerned with making the philosophy visible - so you can decide whether it is the one you want.

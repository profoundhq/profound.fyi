# The Beat Network

A method for sequencing organisational change

---

## Most change programmes fail on sequencing

Most programmes pick reasonable interventions, then sequence them badly or not at all.

The typical plan is parallel workstreams, a Gantt chart that nobody reads, and governance that looks like coordination.

What it lacks is causal logic.

---

## The method borrows the beat sheet from screenwriting

Studios use beat sheets to check that a story works before they commit to production. The structure is flat and sequential, with nowhere to hide.

An organisation has many groups living through change at once, so the Beat Network adds dependencies across groups, conditions on the ground, and observable signals.

---

## Six primitives, no more and no fewer

- **Beat:** one change move, written as one complete sentence
- **Changes:** an ordered line of beats with a name, a scope, and an owner
- **Gate:** a named point where one set of changes waits on another
- **Condition:** the state of the ground a beat needs to work
- **Signal:** observable evidence that a beat has landed
- **Stall:** a signal that does not arrive when expected

---

## Every beat must earn the next

1. Write each beat as a complete sentence, with one job
2. Apply the position test: if you can move a beat without breaking the logic, the bridge is not built
3. Merge beats that do the same thing
4. Give every beat a signal

If you cannot say how you would know a beat has landed, it is not concrete enough.

---

## A stall tells you something

Check the condition before a beat. If the ground is not ready, no amount of upstream progress helps.

When a signal does not arrive, choose one response: wait, re-sequence, rework, or dissolve.

Stalls travel through gates, and the network makes that knock-on effect visible.

---

## Groups move at different speeds, and that is expected

Like players in a multiplayer game, groups progress through their own quest lines and meet only at named convergence points.

Unlike a game, you design the map while you play it.

The network has no durations and no Gantt bars. It says what order things happen in and why. It never says when.

---

## Leadership decides, the network holder makes decisions visible

- **Network holder:** tracks signals, surfaces gates and stalls, and prepares decisions
- **Network authority:** the leadership team, which decides on stalls and approves or dissolves changes

The two meet on a regular cadence, fortnightly in most contexts.

---

## Gates mark where one change depends on another

A product operating model adoption might run three sets of changes: Topology, Sensing, and Capability.

- The pilot team cannot claim faster delivery without a sensing baseline
- Coaching cannot start until a pilot team exists

Getting gates wrong is how programmes produce activity without progress.

---

<!-- _class: steps -->

## Build the network, then run it as an experiment

1. **Name** two to five lines of change
2. **Frame** each with its first and last beat
3. **Fill** the middle and test each beat's position
4. **Connect** beats with gates, conditions, and signals
5. **Run** it and update as beats land or stall

Treat the network as a hypothesis to test.

---

## Ask whether each beat has landed

"Are we on track?" invites false confidence.

"Has this beat landed, and if not, what does that tell us?" invites a decision.

A catalogue of social and technical practices that can support continuous, in-work learning, with an emphasis on learning together. Explore it on your own or with your team.

This is a catalogue, and it is not a playbook. Each entry says what a practice is and what to watch out for. None of them yet says when the practice applies, when to avoid it, or how you would know it is working.

## Practices

### 1-1 coaching

A coach works with one person over a series of conversations to help them think through goals, problems, and their own ways of working. The coach mostly asks rather than tells, so the learning belongs to the person being coached. It suits development that is personal or sensitive, and works best when the person chooses the topic. Keep coaching separate from line management where you can, because people are less candid with someone who also assesses them.

### Asking for help

Sounds straightforward, yet people often avoid asking for help, for many reasons. Look for ways to normalise asking for help in your teams, and make sure the help people receive is actually helpful. You might create dedicated help channels where people can easily connect around a question or problem. You might work to foster a culture of help in your organisation or team, which will most likely need a whole-person approach rather than a focus on work matters alone. You might also give people a base level of teaching and mentoring skills to make them better helpers. Perhaps most importantly, foster a culture of collaboration and teaming, where people instinctively call on each other to solve a problem.

- [How to ask for help](https://www.self.com/story/how-to-ask-for-help) (SELF)
- [Video on asking for help](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sZZHkqIY0Fo) (YouTube)

### Blameless post-mortems

After an incident or failure, the people involved meet to reconstruct what happened, why their actions made sense at the time, and what to change. The aim is to understand the system that allowed the failure rather than to find someone to blame. People only speak openly when they trust the blameless promise, so leaders need to keep it, especially the first time something goes badly wrong. Share the write-up beyond the team so others can learn from it too.

- [Postmortem culture: learning from failure](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/) (Google SRE book)

### Blogging

Writing, internally or in public, about what you are working on and learning. Explaining something to readers forces you to understand it, and a post outlasts the conversation it came from. Team blogs and weeknotes make work visible across an organisation. Lower the bar to publishing, since short posts count, and agree what can be shared outside before anyone writes about client or sensitive work.

### Book clubs

A group reads the same book and meets regularly to discuss it. Discussion turns private reading into shared understanding, and often gives a team a common vocabulary. Pick books that relate to current work, keep the reading per session small so people can keep up, and let people come along even if they have not finished. Rotating who leads the discussion spreads the effort.

### Brainstorming

A group generates many ideas quickly before judging any of them. Separating generating ideas from evaluating them helps people build on each other's thinking. The loudest voices and the first ideas can dominate a group, so consider having people write ideas silently before sharing them, sometimes called brainwriting. Be clear about what will happen to the ideas afterwards, or people stop contributing.

### Chatrooms and chatbots

Chat tools let people ask questions and share what they know in the flow of work. Topic channels make answers searchable, and chatbots can point people to documentation or answer frequent questions. Chat is fast but forgetful, so copy answers that keep coming up into a wiki or other lasting home. Check chatbot answers regularly, because a confident wrong answer teaches the wrong thing.

### Code review

Someone other than the author reads a change before it is merged. As well as catching defects, review spreads knowledge of the codebase and lets people learn from each other's approaches. Keep changes small so reviews are quick, and frame comments as questions or suggestions. Long queues of changes waiting for review slow delivery, and for some teams pairing or mobbing gives the same learning with less delay.

### Communities of practice

People who share a craft or interest across team boundaries meet regularly to learn from each other, for example testers, designers, or people working with a particular technology. They spread good practice and give specialists peers outside their own team. They work best when membership is voluntary and members set the agenda. Give them time and a little support, since communities that run entirely on goodwill tend to fade.

- [Introduction to communities of practice](https://www.wenger-trayner.com/introduction-to-communities-of-practice/) (Etienne and Beverly Wenger-Trayner)

### Conferences

Attending or speaking at conferences exposes people to ideas, tools, and peers beyond their own organisation. The learning often comes as much from corridor conversations as from the talks. Ask attendees to share what they learned when they return, through a lightning talk, a write-up, or a demo, so the value reaches the wider team. Cost and travel can exclude people, so consider online events and rotate who goes.

### Creating shared resources

Teams build learning materials together, such as guides, templates, checklists, diagrams, or example code. Creating the resource is itself a learning activity, because it makes people agree on what they know. Give each resource an owner and a review date so it stays accurate. Start from a real need, since resources created speculatively are rarely used.

### Demos

A team shows working software or other outputs to stakeholders and colleagues, usually at the end of an iteration. Demos create a regular point for feedback and let people outside the team see what is being built and why. Show real, working things in preference to slides, and invite questions. Rotating who presents helps everyone in the team understand the whole.

### Documenting

Writing down how things work, why decisions were taken, and how to do common tasks. Good documentation lets people learn at their own pace and reduces dependence on a few experts. Lightweight formats such as decision records and runbooks are easier to keep current than long manuals. Write for the reader who will need it, and keep documents close to the work, for example alongside the code.

### Dojos

A dojo is a dedicated space and time to practise skills on realistic problems, away from delivery pressure. Coding dojos often use katas, small exercises repeated to build fluency. Some organisations run immersive dojos where a team brings its own backlog and works with coaches for several weeks. Keep the focus on practice over output, and make it safe to try things and fail.

- [Coding Dojo](https://codingdojo.org/) (codingdojo.org)

### Experiments

Treating a change as an experiment, with a hypothesis, a small test, and a way to measure the result. Experiments turn opinions into evidence and help teams learn about their users and their own ways of working. Keep experiments small and cheap, and decide in advance what result would change your mind. Share results that disprove a hypothesis as well as those that support it.

### Game days and simulations

A team rehearses a realistic scenario, such as a system outage or a security incident, in a controlled setting. Practising under realistic conditions exposes gaps in tools, runbooks, and knowledge before a real event does. Start with scenarios everyone knows about in advance, and build up to surprise exercises as confidence grows. Debrief afterwards, as you would after a real incident.

### Giving and receiving feedback

Telling someone how their work or behaviour affected you, and hearing the same about yourself. Regular, specific feedback is one of the quickest ways to learn, but it depends on trust and skill on both sides. Describe what you observed and its effect, and avoid judging the person. Practise receiving feedback with curiosity, and make it a habit instead of something saved up for reviews.

### Group coaching

A coach works with a small group of people, often with similar roles or challenges, who are not necessarily in the same team. Members learn from the coach and from each other's situations. Group coaching reaches more people than one-to-one coaching and builds peer support. Agree confidentiality at the start, and keep the group small enough that everyone has time to speak.

### Group problem-solving

People with different perspectives work on a problem together, using a structured method such as root cause analysis, the five whys, or A3 thinking. Working through a real problem builds shared understanding and problem-solving skill at the same time. A facilitator helps the group stay with the problem instead of jumping to solutions. Involve the people who do the work, since they usually know most about the problem.

### Guided experiences

A guided experience is a curated sequence of tasks that takes a person through a multistage process, possibly spanning several systems. It could support someone through onboarding, running an experiment, or a formal progression process. It is more than a to-do list, because it considers the goals and experience of the person doing the tasks. Guided experiences can be delivered in many ways, from a simple wiki page to a course on a company's learning management system.

### Hack days

A day or two set aside for people to work on ideas of their own choosing, often in small cross-team groups. Hack days let people try new technologies, collaborate with colleagues they rarely work with, and explore ideas that do not fit the roadmap. Finish with demos so the learning is shared. Decide in advance what happens to promising ideas, since repeatedly abandoned work makes people cynical.

### Improvement blitzes

A short, focused burst of effort, typically a few days, in which a cross-functional group improves a specific process or problem. They are also known as kaizen events or rapid improvement events. The group learns by observing the current process, testing changes, and measuring the results. Plan the follow-up before the blitz starts, as improvements often slip back without someone owning them.

- [Kaizen](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/kaizen/) (Lean Enterprise Institute)

### Learning backlog

A visible list of things a person or team wants to learn, managed alongside the work backlog. Making learning goals explicit means they can be prioritised and given time instead of being squeezed into the margins. Review the backlog regularly, for example at retrospectives, and link items to upcoming work where you can. Protect time for it, or it becomes a wish list.

### Lightning talks

A lightning talk is a short talk, usually lasting two to five minutes. Variations include the pecha kucha and Ignite formats. Lightning talks are common at conferences, but they also fit workplace events such as lunch and learns, demos, and learning days. They are a good way to build presentation skills, consolidate and share learning, or invite feedback and discussion on a problem or idea. For the audience, they are a chance to practise active listening, reflective questioning, and giving feedback.

- [What is a pecha kucha presentation?](https://ce.fresno.edu/news/what-is-a-pecha-kucha-presentation) (Fresno Pacific University)
- [How to give a great Ignite talk](https://scottberkun.com/2009/how-to-give-a-great-ignite-talk) (Scott Berkun)

### Lunch and learns

If you are expecting people to give up their lunchtime, choose the activities and format sensitively so people have the chance to eat and draw breath. Set expectations about how people should engage and interact.

- [Lunch and learn](https://www.hoppier.com/blog/lunch-and-learn) (Hoppier)
- [How to run successful lunch and learn events](https://www.mindtools.com/atv697v/how-to-run-successful-lunch-and-learn-events) (MindTools)
- [How to run a world café](https://www.mindtools.com/akolpj7/how-to-run-a-world-cafe) (MindTools)

### Mentoring

A more experienced person supports someone's development through regular conversations, sharing advice, experience, and connections. Mentors offer their own knowledge more directly than coaches do. Mentoring helps people learn the unwritten parts of a role or organisation. Let people choose their mentors where possible, and agree how often you will meet and what you will focus on. Mentors learn too, and reverse mentoring, where junior people mentor senior people, can be just as valuable.

### Mobbing

The whole team works on the same thing, at the same time, at the same computer. One person types while the others think, discuss, and direct, and the roles rotate every few minutes. Mobbing spreads knowledge across the team quickly and avoids hand-offs. It can be tiring, so take regular breaks, and it does not suit every task. It is also known as ensemble or mob programming.

### Observability tools

Logs, metrics, traces, and dashboards show how systems behave in production. Exploring them helps people learn how a system actually works, which is often different from how they think it works. Encourage people to ask questions of the data as well as reacting to alerts. Pairing newer team members with experienced ones during investigations spreads the skill of reading the signals.

### Online courses

Self-paced or scheduled courses delivered online, from short tutorials to full certifications. They make structured learning available on demand and at low cost. Completion rates for self-paced courses are often low, so working through a course with colleagues and applying the material to real work both help. Give people time during working hours to study.

### Pairing

Pairing means working in partnership with someone, in real time, towards the same goal. Developers typically practise it when writing code, but you can pair on non-coding activities too. Pairing has many benefits, not least person-to-person knowledge transfer and diversity of thinking. Consider the challenges of pairing as well, and be mindful about when and how you apply it.

- [On pair programming](https://martinfowler.com/articles/on-pair-programming.html) (Birgitta Böckeler and Nina Siessegger)
- [Supporting neurodiversity in paired programming](https://sorrelharriet.medium.com/supporting-neurodiversity-in-paired-programming-8b250d2b5cab) (Sorrel Harriet)

### Peer review

Colleagues review each other's work, such as documents, designs, plans, or analysis, before it is finalised. Peer review improves quality and lets reviewers and authors learn from each other's thinking. Ask for specific feedback, and keep reviews timely so they do not hold up work. Treat it as collaboration instead of gatekeeping.

### Processing pauses

A processing pause means taking time to think, or simply to be, after an emotionally or cognitively demanding experience. Processing pauses can be worked in and around meetings, workshops, and other situations where you have to react or solve problems in the moment. During a pause you might write things down, reflect on what just happened, or check in with yourself and how you are feeling.

### Reading

Reading books, articles, papers, and documentation to learn independently. Reading is flexible and cheap, and brings in ideas from outside the organisation. Sharing what you read through book clubs, reading lists, or short summaries in a team channel multiplies its value. Make reading during working hours a legitimate use of time.

### Reflective questioning

A powerful learning skill that anyone can use to prompt reflection in others. Used in teams, it encourages reflective inquiry and can also help build trust and diversity of thought. Many tools exist to help develop reflective questioning.

- [The 4As reflective questioning framework](https://www.laas.consulting/resources/4As-reflective-questioning-framework.pdf) (LAAS Consulting, PDF)

### Retrospectives

A team regularly pauses to reflect on how it has been working and agree what to change. Retrospectives are the core learning loop of agile teams. Vary the format to keep them fresh, and make sure everyone can speak. Limit actions to a small number the team will actually complete, and check progress on them at the next retrospective, or people lose faith in the process.

- [Retromat](https://retromat.org/) (activities for retrospectives)

### Shared source code repository

Making code visible across teams so anyone can read it, learn from it, and contribute, an approach often called inner source. Reading other teams' code shows how they solved problems and spreads good practice. Clear contribution guidelines, readable code, and good README files make a repository a learning resource as well as a store of code.

- [InnerSource Commons](https://innersourcecommons.org/)

### Special interest channels

Chat channels dedicated to a topic, technology, or interest, open to anyone in the organisation. They connect people across team boundaries and give them a place to ask questions and share discoveries. A channel needs a few active members to stay useful. Archive channels that have gone quiet so people can find the active ones.

### Swarming

The team stops starting new work and concentrates on finishing a single item, often an urgent problem or a blocked piece of work. Swarming gets work finished sooner and exposes people to parts of the system they do not usually touch. It works best when everyone has a clear role in the swarm. If a team swarms constantly, it may be taking on too much.

### Team coaching

A coach works with a whole team to improve how it works together, including communication, decision-making, and delivery practices. The coach observes the team at work and helps it reflect on what it sees. Team coaching works best when the team has asked for it. Agree goals and an end point, so the team builds its own capability instead of relying on the coach.

### Team health checks

A team regularly rates itself against a set of factors, such as pace, quality, fun, learning, and support, and discusses the results. Health checks start conversations about problems that might otherwise go unspoken, and show trends over time. Use them for discussion within the team. Comparing teams on their scores quickly makes the scores dishonest.

- [Squad health check model](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2014/09/squad-health-check-model) (Spotify Engineering)

### Tests as documentation

Well-written automated tests describe how a system is expected to behave, with examples that stay current because they run. Reading the tests is often the quickest way to understand unfamiliar code. Name tests after the behaviour they check, and write them in the language of the domain. Behaviour-driven development makes this explicit.

- [Behaviour-driven development](https://cucumber.io/docs/bdd/) (Cucumber)

### Today I learned

People share small things they have learned, in a dedicated channel, a shared document, or a regular slot in a meeting. Sharing small lessons normalises learning in public and lets knowledge spread casually. Keep the bar low so people share often, and welcome posts about mistakes and surprises as much as clever discoveries.

### Unconference

A participant-led event where attendees set the agenda on the day. People propose sessions, everyone chooses where to go, and anyone can leave a session that is not useful to them. Open Space Technology is a well-known format. Unconferences bring out the topics people care about and spread learning across an organisation. They need good facilitation and a clear overall theme.

- [What is Open Space Technology?](https://openspaceworld.org/wp2/what-is/) (Open Space World)

### Walk and talks

A walk and talk is a conversation away from the desk. You might combine it with another activity, such as walking the dog or visiting a gallery. The change of setting and the physical movement can prompt fresh thinking and help you avoid the distractions and relational barriers of an office. This format may not suit every topic, and the activity and location should feel comfortable and accessible to everyone taking part.

### Watercooler chats

Informal, unplanned conversations between colleagues. A surprising amount of learning happens in them, as people share problems, tips, and news. Remote and hybrid teams lose many of these moments, so they need to create them deliberately, for example with optional social calls, random coffee pairings, or open video rooms. Keep them optional and free of a work agenda.

### Wiki or knowledge base

A shared, searchable store of information that anyone can read and update. A good wiki lets people find answers for themselves and keeps knowledge when people leave. Wikis decay without care, so give pages owners and dates, and delete what is out of date. Link to it from where people already work, such as chat and the code repository.

### Work shadowing

Spending time observing someone in a different role or team as they do their work. Shadowing builds empathy and understanding of how other parts of the organisation work, and can help people explore a change of career. Agree what the shadow will see and when they can ask questions. Reciprocal shadowing, where both people take a turn, doubles the learning.

### Workshops

A facilitated session where participants work actively on a topic or problem together, instead of listening to a presentation. Workshops suit learning that needs discussion, practice, or a group decision. Plan the outcomes and activities in advance, and leave time to reflect at the end. Capture and share what came out of the session so it lasts beyond the room.

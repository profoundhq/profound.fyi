A catalogue of social and technical practices that can support continuous, in-work learning, with an emphasis on learning together. Explore it on your own or with your team.

This is a catalogue, not a playbook. It names practices but does not yet say when each one applies, when to avoid it, or how you would know it is working. Eight of the 48 practices have a description so far. The rest are named and waiting.

## Described practices

### Asking for help

Sounds straightforward, yet people often avoid asking for help, for many reasons. Look for ways to normalise asking for help in your teams, and make sure the help people receive is actually helpful. You might create dedicated help channels where people can easily connect around a question or problem. You might work to foster a culture of help in your organisation or team, which will most likely need a whole-person approach rather than a focus on work matters alone. You might also give people a base level of teaching and mentoring skills to make them better helpers. Perhaps most importantly, foster a culture of collaboration and teaming, where people instinctively call on each other to solve a problem.

- [How to ask for help](https://www.self.com/story/how-to-ask-for-help) (SELF)
- [Video on asking for help](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sZZHkqIY0Fo) (YouTube)

### Guided experiences

A guided experience is a curated sequence of tasks that takes a person through a multistage process, possibly spanning several systems. It could support someone through onboarding, running an experiment, or a formal progression process. It is more than a to-do list, because it considers the goals and experience of the person doing the tasks. Guided experiences can be delivered in many ways, from a simple wiki page to a course on a company's learning management system.

### Lightning talks

A lightning talk is a short talk, usually lasting two to five minutes. Variations include the pecha kucha and Ignite formats. Lightning talks are common at conferences, but they also fit workplace events such as lunch and learns, demos, and learning days. They are a good way to build presentation skills, consolidate and share learning, or invite feedback and discussion on a problem or idea. For the audience, they are a chance to practise active listening, reflective questioning, and giving feedback.

- [What is a pecha kucha presentation?](https://ce.fresno.edu/news/what-is-a-pecha-kucha-presentation) (Fresno Pacific University)
- [How to give a great Ignite talk](https://scottberkun.com/2009/how-to-give-a-great-ignite-talk) (Scott Berkun)

### Lunch and learns

If you are expecting people to give up their lunchtime, choose the activities and format sensitively so people have the chance to eat and draw breath. Set expectations about how people should engage and interact.

- [Lunch and learn](https://www.hoppier.com/blog/lunch-and-learn) (Hoppier)
- [How to run successful lunch and learn events](https://www.mindtools.com/atv697v/how-to-run-successful-lunch-and-learn-events) (MindTools)
- [How to run a world café](https://www.mindtools.com/akolpj7/how-to-run-a-world-cafe) (MindTools)

### Pairing

Pairing means working in partnership with someone, in real time, towards the same goal. Developers typically practise it when writing code, but you can pair on non-coding activities too. Pairing has many benefits, not least person-to-person knowledge transfer and diversity of thinking. Consider the challenges of pairing as well, and be mindful about when and how you apply it.

- [On pair programming](https://martinfowler.com/articles/on-pair-programming.html) (Birgitta Böckeler and Nina Siessegger)
- [Supporting neurodiversity in paired programming](https://sorrelharriet.medium.com/supporting-neurodiversity-in-paired-programming-8b250d2b5cab) (Sorrel Harriet)

### Processing pauses

A processing pause means taking time to think, or simply to be, after an emotionally or cognitively demanding experience. Processing pauses can be worked in and around meetings, workshops, and other situations where you have to react or solve problems in the moment. During a pause you might write things down, reflect on what just happened, or check in with yourself and how you are feeling.

### Reflective questioning

A powerful learning skill that anyone can use to prompt reflection in others. Used in teams, it encourages reflective inquiry and can also help build trust and diversity of thought. Many tools exist to help develop reflective questioning.

- [The 4As reflective questioning framework](https://www.laas.consulting/resources/4As-reflective-questioning-framework.pdf) (LAAS Consulting, PDF)

### Walk and talks

A walk and talk is a conversation away from the desk. You might combine it with another activity, such as walking the dog or visiting a gallery. The change of setting and the physical movement can prompt fresh thinking and help you avoid the distractions and relational barriers of an office. This format may not suit every topic, and the activity and location should feel comfortable and accessible to everyone taking part.
